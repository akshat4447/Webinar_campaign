Three Layers of LinkedIn Reach

LinkedIn's business APIs cannot message the contacts you already own — no API sends a direct message to a person you pick, and paid targeting needs 300 matched members where your campaigns hold 24–44. What LinkedIn genuinely offers is an event hub you create by API and paid reach to people you don't have yet, with registrants flowing back into the app.

What changed against our assumptions

Three findings that moved the goal. All verified against LinkedIn's own documentation, Marketing version 202608.

We assumed → Message Ads API Discontinued since May 2023. LinkedIn's docs read "We're discontinuing Message Ads, starting May 2023." The live product is Conversation Ads; the inMailContents endpoint survives only as its container.
We assumed → a bulk DM endpoint exists It does not, at any tier. Every candidate is gated or closed, and the one that isn't — the Messages API — requires the member to see and edit each draft and press send themselves. It explicitly forbids automated or scheduled use.
We assumed → upload the CSV and target it A 300-member floor blocks it. Every campaign needs ≥300 members in its audience, and LinkedIn recommends 300,000 for Sponsored Messaging. A 24–44 contact list cannot clear that even at a 100% match rate.

Every messaging API, and why it's out

Checked so this question doesn't get reopened later. Anyone selling "LinkedIn bulk DM automation" is either operating under their own partner agreement or scripting a logged-in browser session — the terms violation we already ruled out.

The three layers

Organised by who you're trying to reach, because that — not the API catalogue — is what decides which tools are even available to you.

Audience — people already in your list (24–44 contacts)

Keep what already works. LinkedIn's API has no role here.

Shipping today

These contacts are unreachable by any LinkedIn API: too few for paid targeting, and no endpoint accepts a named recipient. The one-click assisted flow already built and verified is the ceiling — it copies the personalized draft and opens the recipient's composer, and you press send. Email carries the rest of the cadence.

Audience — anyone who finds the webinar on LinkedIn

Create the LinkedIn Event by API. This is the highest-value piece.

Needs Page admin

A real LinkedIn Event becomes the webinar's hub on the platform, built straight from fields the app already holds — name, description, schedule, Zoom link. Attach a registration form and every signup flows back through Lead Sync as a new contact, gets scored by Claude, and syncs to LeadSquared as a real Lead. No audience floor, no cost per head.

Two constraints worth designing around. The POST /rest/posts step is not optional — until the event is posted it cannot even be fetched or updated. And a registration form can only be attached at the moment of creation and is not editable afterwards, so the form has to be created and approved before the app creates the event.

The gap: LinkedIn has no invite endpoint. Inviting members to an Event is a UI-only action, so a human clicks "Invite connections" once. The app creates, publishes, and harvests — it cannot invite.

Audience — the ICP you don't have contacts for

Paid reach by attribute, not by list. This is demand generation.

Budget + 3 approvals

This is where the 300 floor stops mattering — you target attributes (job title, seniority, industry, company size, location) rather than uploading names, and those audiences run to hundreds of thousands. A Conversation Ad lands in the inbox with a lead-gen form attached; submissions return via Lead Sync as brand-new contacts. It finds people who were never in your CRM, which is the opposite of outreach.

Cost-per-send (CPS), charged per message delivered.

sendTestInMail is the most useful thing in this layer for us: it delivers a test message to your own inbox, with no spend and no real recipient. That makes the whole path verifiable before a single rupee is committed, and it's what the validation layer should gate activation on.

Also true and worth knowing before writing copy: a "Not Interested" button is injected at render time and isn't exposed in the API; replies are constrained to preset option buttons rather than free text; creatives enter a review queue on activation and cannot be modified once live; and in the EEA and Switzerland only members who opted in to Sponsored Messaging are reachable at all.

Your own list, if it ever reaches 300

Matched Audiences remains the right tool for retargeting people you do have — it's simply out of range at current list sizes. Worth building behind a gate rather than not at all.

How a registrant becomes a scored lead

This is the part that makes the integration worth building — it closes the loop into the LeadSquared and Claude work that already exists.

Lead Sync is what carries the inbound half, and it treats organic and paid identically — same endpoint, different owner. Events come back as leadType: EVENT owned by an OrganizationUrn; ad leads as leadType: SPONSORED owned by a SponsoredAccountUrn. It also has a limitedToTestLeads=true filter, which gives us a safe way to exercise the whole pipeline on test submissions before anything real lands in it.

Three approvals, applied for separately

None of these are self-serve, and one does not grant another. This is the real timeline risk — not the engineering.

  1. Advertising API
    rw_ads · r_ads · r_ads_reporting
    Applied for in the Developer Portal. A super admin of your LinkedIn Page must verify the app, and you need an Ad Account with billing configured. New apps land on Development tier — edit rights on a maximum of five ad accounts; unlimited access needs a separate support ticket. Also required for creating the Event registration form.
  2. Lead Sync API
    r_marketing_leadgen_automation
    A separate program — Advertising API approval explicitly does not grant it. This is the one that matters most for us, because without it registrations never reach the app and the whole loop above stays manual.
  3. Matched Audiences API
    rw_dmp_segments
    A third application, with Advertising API approval as a prerequisite. Only needed for retargeting your own list, so it can wait until a campaign audience realistically clears 300.

Five things I could not verify

Stated here rather than assumed anywhere in the design. Each is widely quoted in vendor blogs and absent from LinkedIn's own documentation, so none should be encoded into code, comments, or UI copy as fact.

  • The numeric frequency cap. Docs say only that LinkedIn "strictly controls" how often a member sees Sponsored Messaging. The commonly cited "one per 30 days" is vendor material, not LinkedIn's.
  • Cost per send. There is no published rate card. Third-party figures around $0.30–$0.80 are unverified.
  • Audience match latency. A processing SLA is referenced but never given a number.
  • Whether recipients can reply in free text, as opposed to only the preset option buttons.
  • Approval turnaround. No SLA is published for any of the three programs above.

Recommended build order

Sequenced so that each stage is independently useful and independently verifiable, and so the longest-lead approval starts first.

NowDelete the simulated bot mode. It is the one part of the app that claims to do something it doesn't — a 600 ms timer per contact writing a fake status. Removing it costs nothing and removes the only dishonest surface.
NowFix the audience cap. The LinkedIn queue is capped at ten contacts while the real approved count is displayed beside it, so any audience upload built on it would silently use a subset.
Day oneFile all three applications. Approval is the critical path, not the code. Start Advertising API and Lead Sync immediately; Matched Audiences can follow.
Week 1Build the Events path. Create, publish, list, and pull registrants. Verifiable as soon as Page-admin access exists, with no budget and no audience floor — this is the piece that actually ships usable.
Week 2Build the validation layer. Config preflight, content limits, the 300-member gate, a spend guard modelled on the existing sandbox send guard, and a mandatory sendTestInMail dry run before any creative can go live.
On approvalWire Conversation Ads. Behind the validation layer, defaulting to sandbox so a creative can never be activated by accident.
